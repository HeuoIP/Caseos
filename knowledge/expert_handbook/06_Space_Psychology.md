# 06 Space Psychology

> How people perceive, feel, and behave in physical spaces.
> The Decision Engine uses this document to reason about the
> emotional and social value dimensions, and to explain why a
> candidate supports (or fails to support) the user's experience.

## 1. Purpose

Capture the psychological principles that make a space feel
welcoming, exciting, calming, or alienating. The Decision Engine uses
these principles to score the emotional and social value dimensions
in 03_Value_Taxonomy.md, and to explain the trade-off in plain
language.

## 2. Scope

**In scope**
- Perceptual principles (what people see, hear, and feel).
- Cognitive principles (how people make sense of a space).
- Affective principles (how a space makes people feel).
- Social principles (how a space supports or fails to support
  interaction).

**Out of scope**
- Clinical psychology and therapy.
- Neurodiversity-specific design (covered in inclusive design
  guidelines, not in the general handbook).
- Architectural theory beyond what the engine can operationalise.

## 3. Core Concepts

The document is organised around seven core concepts. Each is a
principle an experienced designer uses intuitively and the engine
must apply explicitly.

1. **Prospect and refuge.** People prefer spaces that offer both a
   view (prospect) and a sheltered nook (refuge). The balance
   depends on the user; a 4-year-old seeks refuge; a 9-year-old
   seeks prospect.
2. **Wayfinding.** People feel safe when they can see the entry,
   the exit, and the path between. A space with hidden corners is
   avoided; a space with clear sightlines is inviting.
3. **Edge effect.** People gather at the boundary between two
   zones, not in the middle of a single zone. Benches at the edge
   of a play area are used; benches in the centre are not.
4. **Personal space.** The radius of comfort varies by culture,
   age, and activity. Crowded play is joyful; crowded rest is
   uncomfortable.
5. **Sensory load.** The combined intensity of sight, sound, touch,
   and motion. Too little load is boring; too much is exhausting.
6. **Affordance.** A space communicates what it is for through
   shape, material, and layout. A low wall affords sitting; a
   slide affords descent; a path affords walking.
7. **Sense of place.** The cumulative impression that "this is
   somewhere, not anywhere." A space without a sense of place is
   forgettable; a strong sense of place is what people remember.

## 4. Heuristics

- **Always offer a refuge.** Even in a thrill-led space, one quiet
  corner is essential.
- **Always show the way out.** A clear exit lowers anxiety even
  when the user has no intention of leaving.
- **Bench at the edge, not the centre.** Place seating where the
  edge effect is strongest.
- **Vary the sensory load.** A space that is uniformly intense is
  uncomfortable; a space that varies is interesting.
- **Make the affordance visible.** A space should not need a sign
  to explain what it is for.
- **Use one strong sense of place per space.** Two competing
  narratives are confusing; one strong narrative is memorable.

## 5. Vocabulary

- **Prospect** — visual or spatial openness.
- **Refuge** — enclosed or sheltered space.
- **Edge** — the boundary between two zones.
- **Affordance** — the perceived possibility for action.
- **Sensory load** — the combined intensity of input channels.
- **Sense of place** — the cumulative identity of a space.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

- **Designing only for prospect.** Open spaces without refuge are
  avoided by younger children, the elderly, and the neurodiverse.
- **Designing only for refuge.** Enclosed spaces without prospect
  feel claustrophobic and are avoided by older children and adults
  on watch.
- **Confusing activity with sensory load.** A busy playground is
  not automatically high-load; a quiet garden is not automatically
  low-load.
- **Letting signage do the work of affordance.** A sign that says
  "this is a quiet zone" is a sign that the quiet zone has failed.
- **Treating "instagrammable" as a sense of place.** A photo
  destination is not a place.

## 8. Worked Example

**Space.** A small plaza near an office building, used at lunch by
office workers.

**Psychological analysis.**
- **Prospect vs refuge.** Office workers seek prospect at lunch
  (open sky, distant view) and refuge from the desk (a nook with
  shade). The plaza needs both.
- **Edge effect.** Workers sit on the plaza edge facing the
  building, not in the centre. A bench at the edge is heavily
  used; one in the centre is ignored.
- **Sensory load.** Mid-day sun and traffic noise are high-load.
  A water feature and a planting buffer lower the load without
  removing the activity.
- **Affordance.** A low wall affords sitting; a raised bed affords
  leaning; a tree affords shade. The plaza should provide all
  three without signs.

**Recommendation.** A linear bench at the building edge, a tree
canopy over part of the plaza, a low water rill along the street
side, and a single landmark object (an IP_Sculpture or a piece of
public art). The plaza's sense of place is "a lunch refuge near
work" — not a destination, not a corridor.
